You said: marx semantic economy

Marxist interpretations of a "semantic economy" explore how meaning, language, and communication are produced, circulated, and exploited like commodities. It bridges Marx's original critique of political economy (how capitalism extracts surplus value from labor) with semiotics (how signs structure human consciousness).

The concept is typically broken down into three core theoretical areas:

1. Language as a Material Resource

In Marxist theory, language is not just a free, abstract medium of thought. It requires material effort—or "semantic labor"—such as time, cognitive strain, and archival maintenance to produce, stabilize, and share meaning. Meaning-making is viewed as the "ontological prerequisite" to economic activity. Before money, trade, or debt can exist, a shared semantic infrastructure and agreement on the meaning of words must already be in place.

2. The Extraction of "Semantic Value"

Building upon this foundation, critical digital labor theorists (like Tiziana Terranova or Christian Fuchs) and contemporary frameworks like the Semantic Economy Institute map Marx's accounting cycle to digital and platform capitalism. Just as a capitalist extracts surplus value from a factory worker's physical labor, platforms and generative AI algorithms are argued to extract surplus value from the digital and cognitive labor of users. These systems capture the "general intellect" (the shared knowledge and communication of society) and convert human interactions and linguistic output into proprietary, monetizable capital.

3. Ideology and the Commodity Fetish

Marx outlined how the cash economy obscures the true social relationships behind production—a concept known as commodity fetishism. Applied to the semantic economy, this suggests that the signs, search results, and cultural narratives generated by digital platforms obscure how they are actually engineered and controlled to benefit capital. As explored in Bob Jessop's analysis of Marx's The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, political or ideological signs can become detached from their material reality, signifying whatever different class forces project onto them to maintain the status quo.
